\title{When Not to Retrain: An Operation-Count Study of Cheap Repairs,
Spurious Alarms, and Delayed Action in Drift Adaptation}

\author{Author names --- United International University}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Retraining a deployed model after a drift alarm is the dominant recurring compute
cost of keeping it alive. Recent work reports energy savings from drift-triggered
retraining, \emph{provided a reliable data change detector is in place}. We
examine that condition. We build a cost-accounted evaluation of what happens
\emph{after} a detector fires: hardware-independent operation counts for both
training and inference, a frozen grid of 500 runs (5 streams $\times$ 4 model
families $\times$ 5 policies $\times$ 5 seeds), a replicate grid under a second
detector, and a full-information oracle that executes every available action at
every alarm. A cost-ordered ``try the cheap repair first'' policy does not win:
it is less accurate than always rebuilding (median $-1.3$ points) and not
reliably cheaper than a fixed schedule, and half of its rebuilds are forced by
its own minimum-window guard. On these benchmarks most of the oracle's advantage
comes from \emph{not acting}: SKIP accounts for roughly 80\% of it, and only
27\% of alarms on the one stream with published change points fall within 1{,}000
rows of one. Inference, not training, is 81--100\% of operations in our setting.
A pre-declared confirm-before-acting rule improves balanced accuracy but fails
our stated cost and safety rules. We report operation counts, never joules.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
concept drift, model retraining, drift detection, cost accounting, measurement study
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

A model in production decays as the data changes. The standard remedy is to
monitor a drift detector and retrain when it fires, for the operational life of
the system. Because that loop repeats, its compute cost accumulates in a way the
initial training cost does not.

Poenaru-Olaru et al.~\cite{poenaruolaru2025} measure this directly and report
that retraining on recent data only can use up to 25\% less energy, and that
drift-triggered retraining can save up to 40\% ``provided a reliable data change
detector is in place.'' That conditional clause is the subject of this paper. We
do not propose a better detector or a better repair. We ask what the condition is
worth: on standard benchmarks, how much of the achievable gain survives when the
alarms themselves are examined, and where the cost actually sits.

Our answer, in one sentence: on these benchmarks most of the achievable gain
comes from \emph{not acting} on alarms, and the cost that dominates is inference,
not training.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A cost-accounted evaluation setup.} Hardware-independent operation
counts for training \emph{and} inference; a frozen 500-run grid (5 streams
$\times$ 4 families $\times$ 5 policies $\times$ 5 seeds) replicated under a
second detector; and a full-information oracle with a per-branch decomposition.
\item \textbf{A negative result for cost-ordered ``try cheap repair first''
adaptation.} The selector is not the best policy; cheap repairs fail in
family-specific ways (inert for Random Forest and Gaussian NB, overwriting for
SGD and XGBoost); and small post-alarm windows trigger guard-forced rebuilds.
\item \textbf{Alarm validity under cost.} Only 27\% of ADWIN alarms on
INSECTS-abrupt fall within 1{,}000 rows of a published change point; 40\%
(ADWIN) and 69\% (DDM) of alarms on Elec2 and Covertype vanish under
block-shuffling; and a no-drift Markov null with matched lag-1 dependence
reproduces the observed falling-error share (0.421 vs 0.416). This extends
Bifet's temporal-dependence result~\cite{bifet2017} to cost; it does not
discover it.
\item \textbf{An oracle decomposition.} About 80\% of the oracle's advantage
comes from SKIP. ``Always SKIP'' captures 95.3\% and 78.1\% of the gap at
$\lambda=10^2$ and $10^3$ but goes negative at $\lambda \geq 10^4$. Oracle
decisions do not transfer across streams from simple features.
\item \textbf{Inference dominates cost} in our setting (81--100\% of
operations). XGBoost warm-start nudges grow the model by $+18.8$ to $+184.9$
operations per prediction, while the add-$k$-retire-$k$ Random Forest nudge is
flat; break-even is near 300 predictions.
\item \textbf{WaitAndCheck}, a supervised instance of the confirm-before-acting
idea of MD3~\cite{sethi2017}, evaluated under rules fixed in advance. The
verdict is \textbf{NO}. Diagnostically it raises balanced accuracy, mostly by
enlarging repair windows rather than by cancelling.
\end{enumerate}

Throughout, results are labelled \textsc{[verdict]} when judged against rules
fixed before the data were seen, and \textsc{[diag]} when they are measurements
that carry no verdict. Thresholds in our decision rules are our own choices, not
standard values.

\section{Background and Related Work}

\textbf{Detectors.} ADWIN~\cite{adwin2007} maintains a variable-length window and
flags a significant change in the monitored error rate in either direction.
DDM~\cite{ddm2004} tracks the cumulative error rate since its own last reset and
fires when it exceeds the best rate seen by three standard deviations. We use
both through river~\cite{river2021}.

\textbf{Energy-aware retraining.} The anchor paper~\cite{poenaruolaru2025}
optimises retraining frequency and data scope and measures training, detection
and inference energy with CodeCarbon for Random Forest. Omar et
al.~\cite{omar2024} measure detector energy only. We count operations instead of
estimating energy, because software energy estimators can err by up to
40\%~\cite{fischer2025}.

\textbf{Benchmark temporal dependence.} Bifet~\cite{bifet2017} showed that a
No-Change classifier is hard to beat on Electricity and Covertype, attributing
this to temporal dependence; see also~\cite{bifet2013,zliobaite2013}. Our alarm
analysis is consistent with that line and adds the cost consequence.

\textbf{Confirming an alarm.} MD3~\cite{sethi2017} labels the next $N$ samples
after a suspected drift and retrains only if $Acc_{Ref}-Acc_{Labeled} >
\Theta\sigma_{Acc}$, discarding the labelled samples otherwise; the idea is
extended in~\cite{sethi2018}. Our WaitAndCheck is a supervised instance of the
same idea and is not claimed as new.

\textbf{Retraining decisions.} CARA~\cite{mahadevan2024} trades staleness
against an abstract retraining cost; CALIPER~\cite{caliper2026} tests post-drift
data sufficiency; Dasari~\cite{dasari2026} finds that with per-sample incremental
updates no retraining policy beats no-retrain by a practically significant margin.
Our position is complementary: we bring operation-level cost accounting over both
training and inference, and an oracle decomposition, to supervised error-rate
detectors.

\subsection{Mechanisms}

Three actions are available at an alarm. \textbf{Skip} returns the model
untouched at zero cost. \textbf{Rebuild} trains a fresh model of the same family
on the full window. \textbf{Nudge} is each family's cheapest incremental update:
append $\lceil 0.05\times100\rceil=5$ boosting rounds for XGBoost; add 5 trees
and retire the 5 oldest for Random Forest, so the forest stays at 100 trees; one
\texttt{partial\_fit} epoch for SGD and Gaussian NB. Measured nudge cost is 4\%
of a rebuild for the tree families, 0.6--1.0\% for SGD and 80\% for Gaussian NB,
whose rebuild is already a single pass and which therefore acts as a negative
control.

Windows frequently lack classes (92\% of 1{,}000-row Covertype windows), so every
\texttt{fit} appends one zero-weight placeholder row per missing class; these
carry no probability mass and are never charged as cost.

\subsection{The selector and the baselines}

\textsc{MechanismSelector} (Fig.~\ref{fig:flow}) splits the alarm window by time
into an older 80\% training part and the newest 20\% as holdout. If the window is
below the minimum (50 holdout or 100 training rows, i.e.\ about 250 rows) it does
not trust any check and rebuilds, recording a \emph{guard-forced} rebuild. Else
it skips if the incoming model is at or above the floor (reference accuracy minus
2 points), otherwise nudges a copy and keeps it if it clears the floor, otherwise
rebuilds and is charged for both. The reference is measured causally on the next
200 rows the stream produces after each adaptation, never on the rebuild's own
training data.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/f1_decision_flow.pdf}
\caption{The selector's decision at one alarm.}
\label{fig:flow}
\end{figure}

Four baselines share \emph{identical} mechanisms and the same temporal split, so
any difference is attributable to the decision rule: NeverAdapt, AlwaysRebuild,
AlwaysNudge and FixedSchedule ($k=3$: nudge, nudge, rebuild).

\subsection{Cost accounting}

Training cost is $\textit{work units} = \textit{passes} \times \textit{rows}$,
with the pass count read from the fitted model (boosting rounds added, trees
added, \texttt{n\_iter\_} epochs, or one for a closed-form fit) rather than
assumed; warm-started fits are counted as a delta against a snapshot taken
before the fit. Inference cost is counted per prediction: tree node visits for
XGBoost and Random Forest (leaf depth per round; \texttt{decision\_path} length
per tree), and parameter reads for SGD and Gaussian NB. Units are comparable
within a family, not across families, and operation counts are not joules.

\subsection{The oracle}

At every alarm on the selector's real trajectory we snapshot the model, window,
reference and position, and execute all four branches (SKIP, NUDGE, REBUILD,
DEFER) from that snapshot, scoring each over a horizon $H$ (rows to the next
alarm, capped at 1{,}000; $H=500$ and $1{,}000$ as sensitivity). Each branch is
charged $\textit{Ops}(a)$ = training + evaluation + inference over the horizon,
and the cost of an action is
\begin{equation}
J(a) = \textit{Ops}(a) + \lambda\,\textit{Errors}(a),
\end{equation}
reported over a grid of $\lambda$ rather than at one value. $O_1$ is the best of
SKIP/NUDGE/REBUILD and $G_1 = \textit{cost(selector)} - \textit{cost}(O_1)$. In a
per-alarm frame DEFER coincides with SKIP, so $G_2$ cannot exceed $G_1$ here;
that limitation is stated rather than worked around.

\subsection{WaitAndCheck and its pre-declared rules}

On an alarm, WaitAndCheck$(W)$ takes no action and keeps predicting for $W$
labelled rows; further alarms are ignored and counted, the detector is not reset,
and the rows enter the buffer. It then scores the model on exactly those $W$ rows
with the floor's metric: if the score is at or above the floor it cancels the
alarm (zero training), otherwise it hands the enlarged buffer to the unchanged
selector. $W=200$ is primary, $W=500$ secondary. The rules, fixed before the runs:
(1) training operations $\leq 0.75\times$ the selector's on $\geq 4$ of 5 streams;
(2) balanced accuracy $\geq$ selector $-1$ point on $\geq 4$ of 5; (3) a hard
safety rule on INSECTS gradual and incremental, where a failure makes the verdict
NO regardless. Elec2 and Covertype motivated the experiment and are therefore not
held-out evidence.

\subsection{Statistics}

XGBoost and Gaussian NB are deterministic at these settings, verified on the
grid, so their five seeds are one observation: blocks are
(stream $\times$ family $\times$ seed) with those two families at seed 0, giving
$n=60$ overall and $n=12$ per stream. We use Friedman with Nemenyi, and paired
Wilcoxon signed-rank with Holm correction; $n=20$ (one observation per cell) is
reported as a stricter check, and conclusions that do not survive it are marked.

\section{Results}

\subsection{The selector does not win \textsc{[diag]}}

Across the 500-run grid, Friedman finds no significant difference between the
five policies in mean prequential accuracy ($p=0.18$ at $n=60$; $p=0.45$ at
$n=20$), while cost separates them sharply ($p=2.9\times10^{-43}$). The selector
is significantly \emph{less} accurate than AlwaysRebuild (median $-1.3$ points,
Holm $p=0.017$) while being much cheaper (cheaper in 51 of 57 non-tied blocks,
rank-biserial $r=-0.96$). It is \emph{more} expensive than FixedSchedule at
$n=60$ (Holm $p=0.0087$), a result that does not survive the stricter $n=20$
check ($p=0.088$) or the DDM replicate ($p=0.065$). Across 20 cells, never
adapting at all was at least as accurate at zero training cost in 10.

Pooling hides the structure: within each stream the policies differ
significantly, with adaptation gaining 20.3 and 36.2 points of median accuracy
over NeverAdapt on INSECTS gradual and incremental and losing 14.8 points on
Covertype. The pooled null is cancellation across streams, a Simpson's-paradox
effect, which is why we report per-stream results throughout.

\subsection{How cheap repair fails \textsc{[diag]}}

Of the selector's 2{,}830 adaptations, 841 were skips, 164 kept nudges and 1{,}825
rebuilds; only 15.2\% of 1{,}079 nudge attempts cleared the floor. Of the 915
failures, 69\% missed by more than 10 points, and in that group the model was
already a median 22.5 points below its floor when the alarm fired.

The failures differ in kind by family, measured at the level of predictions
rather than accuracy: a Random Forest nudge changed \emph{no} prediction in 60\%
of 1{,}876 nudges and Gaussian NB in 77\% of 364, whereas SGD changed a median
9.4\% of predictions for a median accuracy gain of 0.0 points, and only XGBoost
had a positive median gain ($+2.5$ points over 212 nudges). Refitting the nudge on
the full window instead of the older 80\% does not change this (median 0.00
points over 1{,}225 paired alarms; XGBoost $+2.28$).

Half of the selector's rebuilds, 910 of 1{,}825, were forced by its own
minimum-window guard rather than by a failed nudge --- 32\% of all its
adaptations. The runner clears the buffer after each adaptation, so a
closely-following alarm arrives on a window too small to judge.

\subsection{What the alarms are \textsc{[diag]}}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/f2_alarm_validity.pdf}
\caption{ADWIN alarms with the model never changed: observed, under a no-drift
Markov null with the stream's own lag-1 dependence, and after shuffling within
blocks of 50 rows (Elec2 and Covertype only). Log scale.}
\label{fig:alarms}
\end{figure}

On INSECTS-abrupt, whose change points are published~\cite{souza2020}, only
27.4\% of ADWIN alarms fall within 1{,}000 rows of one; the share rises with
tolerance but stays a minority. Destroying local label dependence by shuffling
within blocks of 50 rows removes 40\% of ADWIN alarms and 69\% of DDM alarms on
Elec2 and Covertype, where the error stream's lag-1 autocorrelation is $+0.58$ to
$+0.90$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:alarms}). An i.i.d.\ null produced \emph{no} ADWIN alarms
at all, so we built a two-state Markov null with each cell's own error rate and
autocorrelation and no drift anywhere: it reproduces the observed falling-error
share almost exactly (0.421 against 0.416 observed). The share of alarms that
fire on a falling error rate is therefore what an autocorrelation-driven alarm
population produces.

Testing the model against its own reference rather than comparing raw estimates,
38.1\% of 2{,}515 ADWIN alarms fired while the model was \emph{significantly}
above the reference measured on the 200 rows after the previous adaptation
(36.5\% with an effective-sample-size correction), against the 5\% such a test
yields by construction. Under DDM the same test gives 8.6\% and 3.5\%, which is
indistinguishable from the test's own rate; this is an ADWIN result only. The
difference has a mechanism: DDM compares against the minimum cumulative error
since its own last reset, which sits below our reference error in 79\% of its
alarms by a median 14.7 points.

\subsection{The oracle: most of the gain is not acting \textsc{[diag]}}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/f3_oracle_branches.pdf}
\caption{Where the oracle's advantage over the selector comes from, per stream,
at $\lambda=10^3$, horizon ``next''.}
\label{fig:oracle}
\end{figure}

On Elec2 and Covertype under ADWIN (2{,}595 alarms), $G_1$ is 16.8--25.5\% and
24.8--37.1\% of the selector's cost respectively, across the whole $\lambda$
grid --- far above the 5\% we had set as the threshold for ``no decision model
can help''. Fig.~\ref{fig:oracle} shows where it comes from: SKIP accounts for
80.8\% (Covertype) and 78.9\% (Elec2) of the advantage, NUDGE about 16\% and
REBUILD 3--5\%.

An ``always SKIP'' rule captures 95.3\% of $G_1$ at $\lambda=10^2$ and 78.1\% at
$\lambda=10^3$ (in-sample reference values), but $-3.6\%$ at $\lambda=10^4$: once
errors are priced heavily the choice of action does matter. At that point,
however, a fitted model does not transfer: trained on one stream and tested on
the other it captures $-0.122$ and $+0.162$ of $G_1$ at $\lambda=10^4$. Within a
stream across seeds it holds on Covertype (0.645) but not Elec2 (0.085).

\subsection{Inference dominates the cost \textsc{[diag]}}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/f4_cost_split.pdf}
\caption{Training as a share of total operations, by family and policy, over
whole streams (mean horizon 50{,}279 predictions per run). The remainder is
inference.}
\label{fig:split}
\end{figure*}

Over a whole stream, training is a small fraction of the operations a policy
causes: inference accounts for 81--90\% of the selector's combined operations on
Elec2 and Covertype, and 85--100\% pooled across policies
(Fig.~\ref{fig:split}). Mean inference cost is 779 operations per prediction,
ranging from 9 (Elec2/SGD) to 8{,}046 (a Covertype/XGBoost run).

The mechanism has a direction. For the 32 kept XGBoost nudges (24 of them on
Covertype), the nudge adds $+18.8$ to $+184.9$ operations per prediction, with a
break-even near 300 predictions against a typical gap of 640--17{,}014
predictions to the next adaptation; median net cost is negative on three of four
cells with data. The Random Forest add-$k$-retire-$k$ nudge adds $+4.8$, $+8.0$,
$+1.3$, $-0.2$ and $-2.1$ operations per prediction --- flat, as designed, which
makes it a clean control only once growth is separated from the window-size
effect. The sample is small and we present this as a mechanism with limited
evidence on lifetime net cost.

A related diagnostic: models rebuilt on 1{,}000-row windows are cheaper to query
than the initial model trained on 3{,}624--8{,}000 rows, but a control model
trained on 1{,}000 rows from the \emph{initial} era is equally cheap, so this is a
window-size artifact rather than a property of rebuilding.

\subsection{WaitAndCheck \textsc{[verdict: no]}}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/f5_waitandcheck.pdf}
\caption{WaitAndCheck ($W=200$) against the selector per stream: change in
balanced accuracy (top) and training operations as a ratio (bottom). Grey bars
mark the two streams that motivated the experiment and are not held-out
evidence; dashed lines are our pre-declared thresholds.}
\label{fig:wac}
\end{figure}

The verdict is \textbf{NO}. Rule 1 fails: training operations reach
$\leq 0.75\times$ the selector's on only 2 of 5 streams (Covertype 0.494, Elec2
0.578, INSECTS incremental 0.867, gradual 1.188, abrupt 3.235). Rule 3, the hard
safety rule, also fails: INSECTS-incremental is $-1.07$ points, which is near the
$-1$ point bar but fails the pre-declared rule. Rule 2 passes (4 of 5).

Diagnostically the picture is more favourable and worth stating. Balanced
accuracy rises on 4 of 5 streams ($+1.98$ to $+9.40$ points; median $+2.8$,
Holm $p=0.0004$ pooled), significantly on INSECTS abrupt (Holm $p=0.0024$) and
gradual (Holm $p=0.0098$) per stream. But the gain is not mainly from
cancelling: guard-forced rebuilds collapse from 752 to 40 on Covertype and 143 to
10 on Elec2 because the waited rows enlarge the repair window, and INSECTS-abrupt
is significantly \emph{worse} on training (median $+332{,}806$ work units, Holm
$p=0.0024$) because each adaptation it still takes is over a bigger window.
Cancelling also discards real drift: 23 of 79 cancelled alarms on INSECTS-abrupt
fall within 1{,}000 rows of a published change point. On Covertype it loses to
NeverAdapt by 8.6 points of balanced accuracy.

\section{Discussion}

Four things follow for a practitioner. First, \emph{adapt less}: on these
benchmarks the largest single lever in the oracle is skipping alarms, and never
adapting was at least as accurate as the adaptive policies at zero training cost
in half the cells. Second, \emph{check what the detector is reacting to}: a large
share of alarms here is produced by temporal dependence rather than by concept
change, which is a property of the benchmarks as much as of the detectors.
Third, \emph{count inference}: at realistic horizons it is the dominant term, and
a repair that grows the model can cost more over its lifetime than the training
it saved. Fourth, \emph{repair-window size matters for accuracy}: most of
WaitAndCheck's accuracy gain came from adapting on larger windows, not from
cancelling alarms, which suggests the window the repair sees is worth studying
directly.

\section{Limitations}

Five streams, one configuration of each detector (ADWIN $\delta=0.002$ two-sided
on the raw error stream; DDM at library defaults), and four model families. We
count operations, not joules: our cost numbers cannot be converted to energy, and
training and inference operations are different quantities that we report
separately. Only 164 nudges were kept by the selector and only 32 of those were
XGBoost, so the growth analysis rests on a small sample. The oracle decides per
alarm with foresight and is therefore an upper bound; DEFER coincides with SKIP
in that frame, so the guard question it was meant to answer is untested. The
oracle and its learnability analysis cover two streams only. XGBoost and Gaussian
NB are deterministic at these settings, so the effective sample is 12 blocks per
stream and 60 overall rather than 100. Elec2 and Covertype motivated WaitAndCheck
and are not held-out evidence for it. Inference totals between two measured
endpoints are interpolated from the logged size trajectory. Finally, an
MD3-style comparison under the same grid is reported as a diagnostic only
[TODO: ConfirmMD3 status], and no result here should be read as a statement
about detectors or repairs outside these configurations.

\section{Conclusion}

We measured what happens after a drift alarm, with operation counts for both
training and inference, a frozen grid, and an oracle. A cost-ordered
try-cheap-repair-first policy is not the best choice; most of the oracle's
advantage is in not acting; the alarms themselves are largely explained by
temporal dependence on these benchmarks; and inference, not training, dominates
the cost. A pre-declared confirm-before-acting rule improved accuracy but failed
our cost and safety rules.

Future work: an MD3-style confirmation rule evaluated under the same accounting;
repair-window size as a declared experiment in its own right, since it appears to
drive the accuracy effect we attributed to waiting; and more streams, including
synthetic generators with known change points and stationary controls, where a
false alarm is false by construction.
